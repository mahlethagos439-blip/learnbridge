## Unit 1: Introduction to Biology

### 1.1 Meaning and scope of Biology

**Learning objectives**
- Explain the main ideas in Meaning and scope of Biology.
- Use the concepts to interpret a familiar biological example.
- Apply accurate scientific vocabulary when answering questions.

**Detailed note**
Biology is the scientific study of life and living organisms. It investigates how organisms are structured, obtain and use energy, grow, reproduce, respond to their surroundings, and interact with other organisms. Its scope ranges from molecules and cells to organisms, populations, ecosystems and the biosphere. Botany studies plants, zoology animals, microbiology microscopic life, ecology relationships with the environment, genetics inheritance, and anatomy and physiology structure and function. Biology connects with chemistry to explain reactions in cells, physics to explain movement and energy, mathematics to analyse data, and geography to study distribution of organisms.

**Example / application**
A biologist studying crop disease may observe symptoms, compare healthy and diseased plants, record evidence and test possible causes. Biology is useful in medicine, agriculture, food production, conservation and biotechnology.

### 1.2 Characteristics of living organisms

**Learning objectives**
- Explain the main ideas in Characteristics of living organisms.
- Use the concepts to interpret a familiar biological example.
- Apply accurate scientific vocabulary when answering questions.

**Detailed note**
Living organisms share several characteristics: cellular organization, metabolism, growth and development, response to stimuli, regulation of internal conditions, reproduction, and adaptation across generations. Movement may be obvious, as in an animal walking, or internal, as in materials moving through a plant. Growth means a lasting increase in size or cell number; development includes changes in form and function. Metabolism includes all chemical reactions, including building molecules and breaking them down to release usable energy. No single feature alone always identifies life: a dormant seed may show little visible activity but can resume growth under suitable conditions.

**Example / application**
Compare a growing seedling with a crystal. The seedling is made of cells, carries out metabolism and develops; the crystal may increase in size but does not perform the integrated processes of life.

### 1.3 Scientific methods and tools in Biology

**Learning objectives**
- Explain the main ideas in Scientific methods and tools in Biology.
- Use the concepts to interpret a familiar biological example.
- Apply accurate scientific vocabulary when answering questions.

**Detailed note**
Scientific investigation commonly begins with a question based on observation. A testable hypothesis proposes an explanation, and a prediction states what should happen if the hypothesis is supported. Investigators plan a fair test, identify independent and dependent variables, control other relevant factors, collect repeatable measurements, analyse results and draw a conclusion limited to the evidence. Observations may be qualitative (colour, shape) or quantitative (mass, length, time). Tools include hand lenses, microscopes, thermometers, balances, measuring cylinders and data tables. Safety, accurate labelling, repeated trials and honest reporting are essential.

**Example / application**
To test whether light affects seedling growth, use similar seedlings, keep water, soil and temperature as constant as possible, vary light exposure, measure growth over the same period, repeat the trial and compare averages.

**Learning objectives**

**Detailed note**
Classification groups organisms by shared characteristics and evolutionary relationships. It makes biodiversity easier to study, helps scientists communicate precisely, and supports identification of useful, harmful or endangered organisms. A classification system uses evidence such as cell type, cell organization, nutrition, reproduction, anatomy and molecular similarities. Similar appearance alone can mislead because unrelated organisms may evolve similar adaptations in similar environments. Identification keys use paired contrasting statements to guide a user toward an organism’s name.

**Example / application**

**Learning objectives**

- Use the concepts to interpret a familiar biological example.

**Detailed note**
Organisms may be prokaryotic, whose cells lack a membrane-bound nucleus, or eukaryotic, whose cells contain a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. Unicellular organisms perform life functions in one cell; multicellular organisms have specialized cells. Autotrophs make organic food from inorganic substances, usually using light energy in photosynthesis; heterotrophs obtain organic nutrients from other organisms or their products. Bacteria are prokaryotic. Plants generally have cellulose cell walls and photosynthetic chloroplasts; fungi absorb nutrients and commonly have chitin cell walls; animals ingest food and lack cell walls. Protists are a diverse group of eukaryotes.

### 2.3 Scientific naming and relationships

**Learning objectives**
- Explain the main ideas in Scientific naming and relationships.
- Use the concepts to interpret a familiar biological example.
- Apply accurate scientific vocabulary when answering questions.

**Detailed note**
Binomial nomenclature gives each species a two-part scientific name: genus followed by specific epithet, for example *Homo sapiens*. The genus begins with a capital letter and the specific epithet with a lowercase letter; both are italicized when typed. Scientific names reduce confusion caused by local names that vary between languages or regions. Taxonomic ranks commonly progress from domain to kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species. Organisms placed in more closely related groups generally share more characteristics and a more recent common ancestor.

**Example / application**
When comparing two organisms, record observable traits and consult a reliable identification key; do not assign a scientific name from one superficial resemblance.

**Learning objectives**
- Explain the main ideas in Cell theory and cell diversity.
- Use the concepts to interpret a familiar biological example.

**Detailed note**
Cell theory states that living organisms are made of one or more cells, the cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life, and new cells arise from existing cells. Cells differ in size and shape because their structures suit their functions. A nerve cell has long extensions for communication, a red blood cell is adapted for oxygen transport, and a root hair cell has a long projection that increases absorption area. Cells may be prokaryotic or eukaryotic, and single-celled organisms carry out all life functions within one cell.

**Example / application**
The relationship between structure and function is a key principle: the shape of a cell is meaningful when connected to the job it performs.

### 3.3 Microscopes, magnification and movement of substances

**Learning objectives**
- Explain the main ideas in Microscopes, magnification and movement of substances.
- Use the concepts to interpret a familiar biological example.
- Apply accurate scientific vocabulary when answering questions.

**Detailed note**
A light microscope uses lenses and visible light to magnify a specimen. Total magnification equals eyepiece magnification multiplied by objective magnification. For example, a 10× eyepiece with a 40× objective gives 400× total magnification. Resolution is the ability to distinguish two close points as separate; greater magnification does not automatically mean greater resolution. Diffusion is the net movement of particles from higher to lower concentration. Osmosis is net movement of water through a selectively permeable membrane from a region of higher water potential to lower water potential.

**Example / application**
If a microscope eyepiece is 10× and the objective is 4×, total magnification is 10 × 4 = 40×. Use thin specimens and adjust focus carefully.

## Unit 4: Reproduction

### 4.1 Asexual and sexual reproduction

**Learning objectives**
- Explain the main ideas in Asexual and sexual reproduction.
- Use the concepts to interpret a familiar biological example.
- Apply accurate scientific vocabulary when answering questions.

**Detailed note**
Reproduction produces new organisms and passes genetic information to the next generation. Asexual reproduction usually involves one parent and no fusion of gametes; offspring are genetically very similar to the parent except for mutations. Binary fission is common in bacteria, budding occurs in organisms such as yeast, and vegetative propagation occurs in plants through structures such as runners, bulbs or tubers. Sexual reproduction involves gametes and fertilization, usually producing genetically varied offspring. Variation can help populations respond to changing environments.

**Example / application**
Potato tubers can produce new plants from buds. This is vegetative propagation and preserves many characteristics of the parent plant.

### 4.3 Plant reproduction and seed dispersal

**Learning objectives**
- Explain the main ideas in Plant reproduction and seed dispersal.
- Use the concepts to interpret a familiar biological example.

**Detailed note**
Flowering plants reproduce sexually through flowers. The anther produces pollen; the stigma receives pollen; the style connects stigma and ovary; ovules inside the ovary contain the female reproductive cell. Pollination is transfer of pollen to a stigma, while fertilization is fusion of gametes. After fertilization, the ovule develops into a seed and the ovary commonly develops into a fruit. Seeds may be dispersed by wind, water, animals or explosive mechanisms. Germination requires suitable conditions, commonly water, oxygen and an appropriate temperature.

Organisms survive within ranges of environmental conditions; extreme conditions can limit distribution.
